Mold Exposure Is Making People Sick and Most Doctors Miss It

By Daniel Corbin, DNP, APRN, FNP-C  ·  October 15, 2026  ·  10 min read

Most conversations about mold stop at visible water damage and a runny nose. But mold produces mycotoxins, microscopic chemical compounds that can enter the body through air, food, and skin, and trigger a cascade of symptoms that standard medicine doesn't know how to categorize. Many people with mycotoxin illness spend years being told their labs are normal and their symptoms are in their head.

They're not. And the frustrating part is that the testing exists. Most providers just aren't ordering it.

What Are Mycotoxins?

Mold itself is everywhere. Most of the time, your body handles it. The problem is mycotoxins, toxic compounds that certain mold species produce as a defense mechanism. These chemicals are small enough to cross into the bloodstream, penetrate the gut lining, and in some cases cross the blood-brain barrier.

Some of the most clinically significant mycotoxins include:

  • Ochratoxin A: Produced by Aspergillus and Penicillium molds. Found in water-damaged buildings and contaminated food including coffee and grains. Associated with kidney damage, immune suppression, and neurotoxicity.
  • Trichothecenes: Produced by Stachybotrys, the black mold associated with flooded drywall and chronically wet buildings. Known to impair immune function and damage the gut lining.
  • Aflatoxins: Produced by Aspergillus flavus. Common in grains, peanuts, and tree nuts. One of the most potent natural carcinogens identified.
  • Zearalenone: An estrogen-mimicking mycotoxin found in moldy grains and corn. Can disrupt hormone balance and has been linked to reproductive issues.
  • Citrinin: Found in grains, certain supplements, and fermented foods. Produced by Penicillium and Aspergillus species. Toxic to the kidneys.

Why Some People React and Others Don't

This is the question I hear most often from patients who are sick while their family members feel fine in the same home. The answer is largely genetic.

About 25% of people carry an HLA-DR gene variant that makes them less able to clear biotoxins, including mycotoxins. For these individuals, mycotoxins accumulate rather than being cleared normally, and symptoms compound over time. The other 75% of the population handles most exposures without lasting effects.

Other factors that increase susceptibility include existing gut dysfunction, prior heavy metal exposure, a high overall toxic load, nutrient deficiencies that impair detoxification pathways, and chronic stress, which suppresses the immune function needed to keep these compounds in check.

What Mycotoxin Illness Feels Like

The symptom picture is wide-ranging and overlaps with several other conditions, which is part of why it gets missed so often. Common presentations include:

  • Fatigue that does not improve with rest
  • Brain fog, poor memory, and difficulty concentrating
  • Recurring sinus congestion or upper respiratory symptoms
  • Frequent headaches
  • Joint and muscle pain
  • Mood changes, including anxiety, depression, and irritability
  • Sleep problems
  • Chemical sensitivities and new-onset environmental sensitivities
  • Digestive issues including bloating, cramping, and irregular bowel habits
  • Hormonal disruption, including changes in menstrual cycles or libido

Many people with mycotoxin illness have already been evaluated for Lyme disease, thyroid dysfunction, autoimmune conditions, and depression, with normal or inconclusive results. The missing piece is often a mycotoxin test.

How I Test for It

Standard blood panels do not detect mycotoxins. I use the MycoTOX Profile from Mosaic Diagnostics (formerly Great Plains Laboratory), a urine-based test that screens for 11 mycotoxins across multiple mold species. It is one of the most comprehensive mycotoxin panels available and gives a concrete picture of what the body is carrying.

In addition to the MycoTOX Profile, I check related markers based on the clinical picture. These may include inflammatory markers like CRP and ESR, HLA-DR genotyping when there is a strong suspicion of genetic susceptibility, organic acids testing for gut dysbiosis and mitochondrial function, a comprehensive hormone panel since mycotoxins can disrupt both cortisol and sex hormones, and liver and kidney function since these are the primary organs involved in mycotoxin clearance.

My Approach to Treatment

Treating mycotoxin illness requires a structured, phased approach. The source has to be addressed first. If a person is still being actively exposed, no treatment protocol will hold. That means a thorough mold inspection of the home and workplace, and in some cases temporary relocation during remediation.

From there, my protocol works through mycotoxin elimination, gut repair, immune support, and hormone rebalancing in a sequence the body can tolerate. The specifics are individualized based on what the labs show, what symptoms are present, and how a person is tolerating treatment. The protocol is methodical and staged. Trying to accelerate it tends to backfire.

The encouraging part: most people see meaningful improvement when the source is removed and the right protocol is in place. Fatigue lifts. Brain fog clears. Sleep improves. Hormones restabilize. The timeline varies, but this is not a condition you are stuck with.

Signs to Watch For

Mold Might Be the Missing Piece If...

  • Your symptoms got worse after moving, or improve when you are away from home or work for a few days
  • You have multiple unexplained symptoms that don't fit a single diagnosis
  • Your labs keep coming back normal despite feeling terrible
  • A prior Lyme, autoimmune, or psychiatric workup was inconclusive
  • You have a history of water damage, flooding, or musty smells in your home or workplace
  • You feel noticeably worse in humid environments or during wet seasons
