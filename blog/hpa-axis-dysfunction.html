Adrenal Fatigue Is Real.
We Just Have a Better Name for It.

If you're exhausted no matter how much you sleep, wired at night but useless in the morning, and running on caffeine and willpower: this is for you.

By Daniel Corbin, DNP, APRN, FNP-C September 1, 2026 10 min read
Functional Medicine Hormones Energy DUTCH Test Raleigh NC

You've Googled "adrenal fatigue." Maybe you even mentioned it to your doctor and got told it isn't a real thing. They ran a lab, said everything looked fine, and sent you home still exhausted.

Here's what I want you to hear: what you're feeling is real. "Adrenal fatigue" isn't the most accurate term, but it points to something that absolutely exists. The medical name for it is HPA axis dysfunction. And it's something I actually test for and treat.

What this post covers: What the HPA axis is, what goes wrong, why your labs might miss it, how to test it properly, and what actually helps.

What Is the HPA Axis?

Think of the HPA axis as a group chat between three parts of your body: your brain, a small gland at the base of your brain called the pituitary, and your adrenal glands (two small glands that sit on top of your kidneys). Together, they manage your stress hormone, cortisol.

Here's how it works when everything is healthy:

  • Your brain senses stress and sends out a signal
  • The pituitary picks up that signal and passes it along
  • Your adrenal glands get the message and release cortisol
  • Once the stressor passes, cortisol tells the brain to calm down and stop signaling

In a healthy person, cortisol follows a natural daily pattern: it's high in the morning to help you wake up and get going, then slowly drops throughout the day, and is at its lowest at night so you can sleep. When that pattern gets disrupted, you feel it everywhere.

So What Does "Adrenal Fatigue" Actually Mean?

"Adrenal fatigue" became a popular way to describe something a lot of people experience: bone-deep tiredness that sleep doesn't fix, salt and sugar cravings, struggling to get out of bed in the morning, crashing hard in the afternoon, and somehow feeling wired when it's finally time to sleep.

The reason doctors push back on the term is that your adrenal glands aren't actually failing (that's a separate condition called Addison's disease). What's really going wrong is the communication system. Your brain and adrenal glands have stopped sending and receiving signals correctly.

"Your adrenals aren't broken. The conversation between your brain and your body has broken down. That distinction matters a lot for how we treat it."

That's why I use the term HPA axis dysfunction. It tells us exactly where the problem is and what to do about it.

What Causes It?

Your stress system is tough, but it has limits. Here are the most common things that push it over the edge:

Long-term stress

Work, money, relationships, being a caregiver. Your body treats all of it the same way it treats physical danger. When stress is constant and never lets up, the system eventually burns out.

Poor sleep

Cortisol and sleep are directly connected. Bad sleep raises cortisol, and high evening cortisol makes sleep worse. Once that loop starts, it's hard to break on your own.

Blood sugar crashes

Every time your blood sugar drops, your adrenal glands have to step in and raise it back up. If you're skipping meals, eating a lot of sugar, or have insulin resistance, your adrenals are doing that job over and over all day long.

Ongoing inflammation

Gut problems, food sensitivities, autoimmune conditions, mold exposure. All of these keep your body in a low-grade state of alarm, which keeps the stress system fired up.

Too much exercise without enough recovery

Hard workouts are a form of stress. That's not a bad thing in the right doses. But if you're already depleted and pushing through intense training, it makes things worse, not better.

Past trauma

Difficult experiences from earlier in life can actually rewire the way the stress system works. This is well-supported by research, and it's something conventional medicine tends to skip over entirely.

Why Your Blood Test Came Back "Normal"

I hear this constantly. Someone feels awful, asks about their cortisol, gets a blood test, and is told everything is fine. They leave more frustrated than before.

The problem is how conventional medicine tests cortisol: a single blood draw, usually in the morning. That one number tells you almost nothing. Here's why:

  • Cortisol changes a lot throughout the day. One snapshot can't show you the pattern.
  • Getting your blood drawn is stressful. It can temporarily spike cortisol, making results look normal even when they're not.
  • The "normal" range on standard labs is very wide. You can have a real problem and still land inside it.
  • Standard labs don't measure the things that actually matter: how cortisol behaves from morning to night, how much is active vs. broken down, and how your other hormones are affected.

Bottom line: A normal cortisol blood test does not mean your HPA axis is working properly. It just means you haven't been tested the right way yet.

The Right Way to Test: The DUTCH Test

The best way to evaluate HPA axis function is with the DUTCH test (Dried Urine Test for Comprehensive Hormones). Instead of a single blood draw, it measures your cortisol at multiple points throughout the day using urine samples you collect at home. This gives a full picture of how your cortisol actually behaves from morning to night.

Here's what it shows that standard labs can't:

  • Morning cortisol surge: In a healthy person, cortisol jumps up in the first 30-60 minutes after waking. If that spike is too flat or too extreme, it's one of the clearest signs something is off.
  • Daily cortisol pattern: Does your cortisol drop like it should throughout the day, or is it flat, inverted, or all over the place?
  • How your cortisol is being used: Are your adrenals making enough? Is your body burning through it too fast?
  • DHEA levels: DHEA is sometimes called the "anti-stress hormone." It tends to drop when your stress system has been overworked for a long time.
  • Sex hormones: Stress hormones and sex hormones (testosterone, estrogen, progesterone) share the same building blocks. When your stress system is out of balance, your sex hormones often are too.

I offer DUTCH testing as part of a full functional medicine evaluation at Staywell Health. The results don't just tell me something is wrong. They tell me exactly what's wrong and where, which completely changes how I treat it.

Does This Sound Like You?

HPA axis dysfunction looks different in different people depending on how the cortisol pattern is disrupted. Some people run high, some run low, and some have a mixed or backwards pattern. Common signs include:

  • Waking up exhausted even after a full night of sleep
  • Needing caffeine just to function in the morning
  • Hitting a wall in the early afternoon (the 2-4 PM slump is a classic sign)
  • Getting a burst of energy at night when you should be winding down
  • Lying in bed tired but unable to fall asleep
  • Craving salt or sugar throughout the day
  • Brain fog, trouble focusing, forgetting things
  • Getting irritated or overwhelmed by things that wouldn't normally bother you
  • Getting sick more often than you used to
  • Low sex drive, irregular periods, or worsening PMS
  • Weight gain around your midsection with no clear reason
  • Feeling worse after exercise instead of better

You don't need every symptom on that list. If several of them feel familiar and your DUTCH results confirm the pattern, that's enough to build a real treatment plan around.

What Actually Helps

The goal isn't to mask symptoms with stimulants or pile on supplements. The goal is to help your stress system find its natural rhythm again. That takes a few things working together at the same time.

1. Sleep as Medicine

I'm not just telling you to sleep more. What matters is when you sleep and how consistent you are. Getting to bed before midnight, dimming lights in the evening, and waking up at the same time every day can shift your cortisol pattern significantly, often within just a few weeks.

2. Keeping Blood Sugar Stable

Eating balanced meals with protein, fat, and fiber at regular times throughout the day means your adrenal glands don't have to keep rescuing your blood sugar. This simple shift can have a big impact on afternoon energy crashes. I also check for insulin resistance in patients dealing with HPA axis issues because the two often go hand in hand.

3. Smarter Exercise

Intense workouts are a cortisol trigger. While you're recovering, I usually recommend pulling back to moderate movement: walks, yoga, swimming, or lighter strength training. You stay active without pushing a system that's already struggling.

4. Adaptogens

Adaptogens are plants that help your body handle stress more effectively. They don't stimulate or sedate, they help regulate. The research behind several of them is solid:

  • Ashwagandha: Multiple clinical trials show it lowers cortisol, reduces perceived stress, and helps with fatigue. It also supports thyroid function and testosterone in men.
  • Rhodiola: Particularly helpful for the "wired but tired" pattern. It reduces mental exhaustion and helps with mood and stress resilience.
  • Eleuthero (Siberian ginseng): Supports physical and mental stamina under stress and has a good body of research behind it, especially in high-demand occupational settings.
  • Phosphatidylserine: A compound that helps reduce the cortisol spike triggered by physical stress and supports focus and memory.

I don't recommend specific supplements without seeing your actual DUTCH results first. Knowing your pattern lets me pick the right tools instead of guessing.

5. Replenishing Key Nutrients

Your adrenal glands use a lot of vitamin C. Under chronic stress, vitamin C, B vitamins (especially B5 and B6), magnesium, and zinc all get used up faster than normal. Getting these back through food and targeted supplementation is an important part of recovery.

I test levels before recommending anything in higher doses. Taking a lot of a nutrient you don't actually need isn't just a waste of money. In some cases it can cause problems.

6. Calming the Nervous System

No supplement will fully fix HPA axis dysfunction if the underlying stress is still active or if your nervous system is stuck in high-alert mode. I work with patients on practical techniques like deep breathing, heart rate variability training, and setting boundaries around work and energy output. When past trauma is a factor, I refer to therapists who specialize in that area.

7. Looking at the Bigger Picture

Depending on what your DUTCH test and full history show, I may also look at:

  • Gut health, since the gut and the stress system directly affect each other
  • Thyroid function, which is tightly connected to adrenal health
  • Hormone imbalances in testosterone, estrogen, and progesterone
  • Toxin exposure like mold or heavy metals that drive ongoing inflammation

A note on low-dose cortisol replacement: In cases where testing confirms a significant cortisol deficiency and other approaches haven't been enough, low-dose bioidentical hydrocortisone can be appropriate under careful supervision. This is not the same as prednisone or other high-dose steroids used for inflammation. It's a tool I use rarely and only when the DUTCH test clearly supports it.

How Long Does It Take to Feel Better?

Honestly, it depends on how long the problem has been building and how much you're able to change. Most of my patients notice real improvement in energy and sleep within 6 to 12 weeks of starting a targeted plan. Full recovery can take anywhere from 6 to 18 months.

The encouraging part is that your body is designed to bounce back. The HPA axis is built to adapt. With the right support and fewer things pulling it off course, most people make meaningful, lasting progress.

"You're not just tired. You're running a system that's been pushed too hard for too long. Recovery is possible, and it starts with figuring out what's actually going on."

You Don't Have to Keep Running on Empty

If a doctor has told you your labs are normal but you still feel terrible, I want you to know that "normal" and "optimal" are not the same thing. There's a way to get a real answer, and there's a path forward from here.
