The Silent Driver Behind Fatigue, Weight Gain, Brain Fog, and Hormone Problems

One condition could be at the root of everything you're struggling with. Most people have never been tested for it.

By Daniel Corbin, DNP, APRN, FNP-C September 15, 2026 10 min read
Metabolic Health Blood Sugar Functional Medicine Hormones Raleigh NC

You're tired. Not just "I need an extra coffee" tired. We're talking about dragging yourself through the day tired. Your weight has crept up even though you haven't really changed what you eat. Your mind feels foggy in the afternoon. Your hormones are off. You've been told your labs are normal, but you don't feel normal.

There's one condition that can quietly cause all of those things at the same time: insulin resistance.

It affects an estimated 40% of American adults. Most of them don't know they have it, because standard lab work often misses it until it's gotten much worse. By the time someone gets a diabetes diagnosis, the underlying problem has usually been building for a decade or more.

This post is about catching it early, understanding what it's doing to your body, and fixing it at the root cause.

What this post covers: What insulin resistance is, why it causes such a wide range of symptoms, how I actually test for it, and what a real treatment plan looks like.

What Is Insulin, and What Goes Wrong?

When you eat, especially carbohydrates and sugar, your blood glucose rises. Your pancreas responds by releasing insulin, a hormone whose job is to act like a key. It unlocks your cells so they can absorb glucose and use it for energy.

In a healthy person, that system works smoothly: glucose goes up, insulin is released, cells open up, glucose gets used, levels come back down.

Insulin resistance happens when your cells stop responding to that key properly. They become resistant to insulin's signal. Your pancreas responds the way any reasonable system would: it makes more insulin and sends it louder. For a while, this works well enough to keep blood sugar in the "normal" range. But your insulin levels are now chronically elevated, and that causes problems far beyond blood sugar.

"By the time blood sugar starts rising, insulin resistance has often been present for years. That's why catching it early requires looking at insulin, not just glucose."

Why Insulin Resistance Causes So Many Different Problems

Insulin isn't just a blood sugar hormone. It talks to almost every system in your body. When insulin stays elevated for too long, the downstream effects reach places most people would never connect to blood sugar.

Fatigue

When your cells resist insulin, they can't take in glucose efficiently. Even though glucose is technically available in your blood, your cells are starved for energy. You feel it as a constant heaviness or exhaustion that doesn't improve with rest. The afternoon crash around 2-4 PM is a classic sign. Your blood sugar drops, your body scrambles to compensate, and your energy tanks.

Weight gain, especially around the middle

High insulin directly tells your body to store fat, particularly around your midsection. It also blocks your body's ability to burn fat as fuel. This is why people with insulin resistance can be doing "everything right" with diet and exercise and still not lose weight. The hormonal environment isn't set up for it. Belly fat, in turn, makes insulin resistance worse. It's a cycle.

Brain fog and trouble focusing

Your brain runs almost entirely on glucose. When glucose uptake is impaired, your brain is among the first to suffer. Research now refers to Alzheimer's disease as "type 3 diabetes" in some circles because of how closely insulin resistance in the brain is connected to cognitive decline. Even in its early stages, insulin resistance can cause that familiar foggy, slow-thinking feeling that makes it hard to concentrate or remember things.

Hormone disruption

This is where it gets interesting. High insulin directly raises androgen (male hormone) levels in women, which is a major driver of PCOS, irregular periods, excess hair growth, and acne. In men, insulin resistance lowers testosterone and raises estrogen. It also disrupts thyroid function and interferes with the HPA axis (your stress hormone system). If you're being treated for a hormone problem but no one has checked your insulin levels, you may be treating a downstream symptom while the real driver stays active.

High blood pressure and heart risk

Insulin causes your kidneys to hold onto sodium, which raises blood pressure. It also promotes inflammation in your blood vessels and drives up triglycerides while pushing HDL (the good cholesterol) down. This is why metabolic syndrome and cardiovascular risk are so tightly connected to insulin resistance.

Mood and anxiety

Blood sugar swings directly affect neurotransmitters. When glucose crashes, adrenaline and cortisol spike to bring it back up. That surge feels like anxiety. Over time, poor metabolic health is linked to higher rates of depression, irritability, and mood instability.

How I Actually Test for It

Here's the problem with standard lab panels: they check fasting glucose and sometimes HbA1c (a 3-month average of blood sugar). Both of those can look completely normal while insulin resistance has been quietly progressing for years.

Think of it this way: fasting glucose and A1c only tell you what blood sugar is doing. They say nothing about how hard your pancreas is working to keep it there. When insulin is high and blood sugar is still normal, the system is compensating, but it's under strain.

The labs I actually use to catch insulin resistance early:

Fasting Insulin

This is the most important test most doctors never order. A fasting insulin level tells you how much insulin your pancreas is pumping out to maintain normal blood sugar. Optimal is generally below 5-8 mIU/L. Many people walking around with "normal" blood sugar have fasting insulin levels of 15, 20, or higher. That's early insulin resistance.

HOMA-IR

HOMA-IR (Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance) is a calculation using fasting insulin and fasting glucose together. It gives a clearer picture of how resistant your cells actually are. I calculate it from your lab values. A result above 2.0 starts raising flags. Above 2.9 is consistent with insulin resistance.

Fasting Glucose

Still useful, but context matters. Optimal fasting glucose is 70-85 mg/dL. Standard labs flag anything under 100 as "normal," but a level creeping into the 90s alongside a high fasting insulin tells a different story.

HbA1c

A 3-month average of blood sugar. Optimal is below 5.3%. Again, a level of 5.6% might fall within "normal" on a standard panel, but combined with the rest of the picture, it tells me the trend is heading in the wrong direction.

C-peptide

C-peptide is released alongside insulin and reflects how much insulin your pancreas is actually producing. It's helpful for distinguishing between early-stage insulin resistance and situations where the pancreas may be producing less insulin overall. I use it selectively based on the full picture.

Fructosamine

Fructosamine reflects blood sugar control over the past 2-3 weeks, compared to HbA1c's 3-month window. I reach for it when I want a shorter-term snapshot, for example when tracking a patient's response to a dietary change or when HbA1c results may be skewed by certain conditions.

Note on oral glucose tolerance testing: A formal oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) can provide additional detail about how your blood sugar and insulin respond to a glucose challenge. I use this selectively when the picture from the panel above is unclear or when more precise characterization of the response curve is needed. For most people, the fasting panel gives me what I need to act on.

Does This Sound Like You?

Insulin resistance doesn't always look like pre-diabetes. Here are some of the signs I see most often in patients who turn out to have it:

  • Energy crashes in the afternoon, especially 1-3 hours after eating
  • Intense sugar or carbohydrate cravings, especially after meals
  • Difficulty losing weight despite reasonable effort
  • Weight gain concentrated in the belly and love handles
  • Brain fog that gets worse as the day goes on
  • Feeling shaky, irritable, or anxious when meals are delayed
  • Frequent hunger even after eating
  • Dark patches of skin around the neck, armpits, or groin (this is called acanthosis nigricans and is a visible sign of high insulin)
  • High triglycerides or low HDL on bloodwork
  • PCOS, irregular periods, or high androgens in women
  • Low testosterone or high estrogen in men
  • Blood pressure creeping up over time

You don't need all of these to have insulin resistance. Some people have it with very subtle symptoms until it's been present for years.

What Actually Fixes It

The good news is that insulin resistance is one of the most reversible conditions I work with. The body responds well to the right inputs. Here's how I approach it.

1. Change What You Eat (Specifically)

You don't have to go zero-carb or follow a rigid diet plan. The goal is to reduce what spikes insulin the hardest and most frequently. That means pulling back on refined carbohydrates (bread, pasta, rice, crackers, anything made with white flour) and added sugars. Replacing them with protein, healthy fats, fiber-rich vegetables, and whole food sources of carbohydrates makes a significant difference for most people within weeks.

Eating protein and fat at the beginning of a meal before carbohydrates is a small shift that measurably blunts the insulin spike from that meal. Food order actually matters.

2. Move Your Body After Meals

A 10-15 minute walk after eating is one of the most effective blood sugar interventions that exists. Muscle contraction during movement pulls glucose out of the bloodstream without needing insulin to do it. This is not about burning calories. It's about using a biological shortcut that bypasses the insulin problem entirely.

3. Strength Training

Muscle is the largest glucose disposal organ in the body. The more muscle you have, the more glucose your body can clear without needing as much insulin. Resistance training 2-3 times per week is one of the most powerful long-term treatments for insulin resistance. Its effects are durable in a way that cardio alone is not.

4. Prioritize Sleep

One night of poor sleep measurably worsens insulin sensitivity the next day. Chronic sleep deprivation is a direct driver of metabolic dysfunction. This isn't a soft lifestyle recommendation. It's physiology. If you're sleeping 5-6 hours and wondering why nothing else is working, sleep is likely a significant part of the problem.

5. Reduce Chronic Stress

Cortisol (your stress hormone) raises blood sugar. Chronically elevated cortisol means chronically elevated insulin. Stress management is metabolic medicine. This is one of the reasons I often look at both HPA axis function and insulin levels together, because they interact directly.

6. Targeted Supplements

Several compounds have solid research behind them for improving insulin sensitivity. I use these based on individual lab results and response, not as a blanket recommendation:

  • Berberine: One of the most well-studied natural compounds for blood sugar and insulin sensitivity. Multiple clinical trials show effects comparable to metformin, with additional benefits for lipids and gut microbiome. It works through several mechanisms, including activating AMPK, an enzyme that helps cells take up glucose more efficiently.
  • Magnesium: Low magnesium is consistently associated with insulin resistance. Magnesium plays a direct role in insulin signaling. I test levels before supplementing.
  • Alpha-lipoic acid (ALA): An antioxidant that improves glucose uptake and reduces oxidative stress associated with high blood sugar. Particularly useful for people with neuropathy symptoms.
  • Chromium: Supports insulin receptor function. Evidence is moderate but consistent, particularly for reducing carbohydrate cravings and improving glucose metabolism.
  • Inositol (specifically myo-inositol): Well-studied for insulin resistance in the context of PCOS, with good evidence for improving insulin sensitivity and hormone balance in women.
  • Gymnema sylvestre: An herb used in Ayurvedic medicine with solid modern research behind it. Gymnema helps reduce sugar absorption in the intestines, supports healthy insulin secretion, and can reduce sugar cravings. Some studies show it helps regenerate insulin-producing cells in the pancreas over time.

I check relevant levels before recommending these and monitor response over time. The goal is targeted correction, not guessing.

7. Intermittent Fasting (When Appropriate)

Giving your body longer breaks between meals naturally lowers insulin levels during the fasting window. A 12-16 hour overnight fast is a reasonable starting point for most people and can be an effective tool for improving insulin sensitivity over time. This approach works well for some people and isn't right for others. I assess it case by case, especially for patients with a history of disordered eating or who are managing other hormonal conditions.

A Note on Medications

For some patients, lifestyle and supplement approaches alone aren't enough or need support while we work on the underlying causes. In those situations, I may recommend:

  • Metformin: The oldest and most studied insulin-sensitizing medication. It works primarily by reducing glucose production in the liver and improving cellular insulin response. It also has emerging evidence for longevity benefits. It's a reasonable option when lifestyle changes aren't moving the needle fast enough, especially at lower doses.
  • GLP-1 receptor agonists (such as semaglutide): These medications reduce appetite, slow digestion, and improve insulin secretion. They've shown significant benefits for blood sugar, weight, and cardiovascular risk. They're a powerful tool and an appropriate one in the right clinical situation. I don't use them as a first line when lifestyle and integrative approaches haven't been tried. But when they're indicated, I'm not opposed to them.

Medications work best when they're part of a broader plan, not a substitute for one. My goal is always to address the root cause so that over time, less intervention is needed.

How Long Does It Take?

Most people start noticing real changes in energy and hunger within 2-4 weeks of improving their diet and adding movement. Measurable improvements in fasting insulin and HOMA-IR typically show up within 8-12 weeks of consistent changes.

Full reversal depends on how long the problem has been present and how completely the contributing factors are addressed. But I'll say this: insulin resistance responds to lifestyle intervention faster than almost any other chronic condition I treat. The body is ready to change. It just needs the right conditions.

"Insulin resistance is not a life sentence. It's a signal that something needs to change, and when you make those changes, your body responds."
