Sperm Health Is Half the Equation and Almost Nobody Is Checking It

By Daniel Corbin, DNP, APRN, FNP-C  ·  November 1, 2026  ·  10 min read

When someone is struggling to conceive, the default in most medical settings is to start with the person who will carry the pregnancy. They get the bloodwork, the imaging, the specialist referrals. Meanwhile, their partner sits in the waiting room.

That approach has a real problem: factors related to sperm contribute to about half of all infertility cases. And in a significant portion of those cases, the sperm has never been evaluated at all.

I see this pattern regularly. One partner has been through months of workups, sometimes invasive procedures, sometimes the beginning of an IVF consultation, and the sperm has never been analyzed. When we finally look, there's the answer.

Sperm health is not a niche concern. It's half of every conception, and it deserves the same attention.

Why Sperm Quality Matters More Than Most People Think

Most people assume fertility comes down to one question: is there sperm? But sperm quality is far more complex than just count.

The three things a semen analysis measures are count (how many), motility (how many are swimming and in the right direction), and morphology (how many are shaped normally). All three matter. A high sperm count means little if 95% of those sperm are abnormally shaped or swimming in circles.

Beyond the basic semen analysis, DNA fragmentation is a piece most providers never order. This test measures how much damage exists in the genetic material inside the sperm. High DNA fragmentation can cause implantation failure, early miscarriage, and failed IVF cycles even when a basic semen analysis looks acceptable. It's one of the most overlooked variables in any fertility workup.

What Actually Damages Sperm

Sperm are produced continuously in a 74-day cycle. That's both a challenge and an opportunity. Whatever is harming sperm quality today will show up in a semen analysis about two and a half months from now. But changes made today can meaningfully improve the next cycle of sperm being produced.

Common contributors to poor sperm quality include:

Oxidative stress. Sperm are highly vulnerable to oxidative damage. The testicles have limited antioxidant defenses compared to other tissues, and when the body is under oxidative stress from inflammation, poor diet, environmental toxins, or chronic illness, sperm quality takes the hit.

Heat exposure. Sperm production requires a temperature slightly below core body temperature, which is why the testicles are external. Frequent hot tub use, prolonged laptop use in the lap, long periods of sitting, and tight underwear all raise scrotal temperature and suppress sperm production.

Hormone imbalance. Testosterone, FSH, LH, and estradiol all have to be in the right balance for optimal sperm production. High estrogen is a particularly common and underappreciated problem. Estrogen tells the brain that testosterone is high enough, which suppresses the hormonal signals that drive sperm production. People who carry more body fat, consume significant alcohol, or have been exposed to environmental estrogens found in plastics and pesticides often have an estrogen-to-testosterone ratio that quietly suppresses fertility.

Varicocele. A varicocele is an enlargement of the veins inside the scrotum, similar to a varicose vein. It raises scrotal temperature and creates oxidative stress around the testicles. Varicoceles are present in about 15% of people overall and in about 40% of those with fertility problems. They're often painless and go undetected without a physical exam.

Infections and inflammation. Prior STIs, chronic prostatitis, and subclinical genital tract infections can all impair sperm quality and function. A standard semen analysis won't reveal these.

Environmental toxins. Pesticides, heavy metals, BPA and phthalates from plastics, and certain industrial chemicals are endocrine disruptors that interfere directly with sperm production and hormone balance. Mycotoxin exposure from mold can also suppress testosterone and impair fertility.

Medications and substances. Anabolic steroids and exogenous testosterone are among the most common causes of impaired sperm production. Testosterone replacement therapy shuts down the body's own hormone signaling, which is the same signaling that drives sperm production. Recreational drugs, heavy alcohol use, and some common medications including certain antidepressants, antifungals, and blood pressure drugs can also impair fertility.

Gender-affirming hormone therapy. For transgender women and gender-diverse people who have been on estrogen-based hormone therapy, sperm production is typically suppressed during treatment. If biological parenthood is a goal, fertility preservation before or during a pause in hormone therapy is an important conversation to have with your provider.

How I Evaluate Sperm Health

A complete workup at Staywell goes well beyond a basic semen analysis. Here's what I look at:

Semen analysis including count, motility, and morphology. The starting point, but not the whole picture.

Sperm DNA fragmentation to assess genetic material integrity. This is the test most providers skip and one of the most clinically important, especially in cases of unexplained infertility or recurrent pregnancy loss.

Hormone panel including total testosterone, free testosterone, estradiol, FSH, LH, prolactin, and SHBG. This tells us whether the hormonal environment is supporting sperm production or working against it.

Thyroid function. Thyroid hormone plays a direct role in sperm production and motility. Hypothyroidism is an underappreciated and completely correctable cause of fertility problems.

Metabolic markers. Blood sugar, insulin, and inflammatory markers. Insulin resistance suppresses testosterone and raises estrogen, a double hit on fertility.

Nutrient levels. Certain vitamin and mineral deficiencies are directly involved in sperm production and DNA integrity and are common enough that I check them routinely.

Physical exam. A clinical exam to check for varicocele, which won't show up on any lab test.

What Actually Helps

Because sperm are produced in continuous cycles, the body has a real ability to recover when underlying problems are addressed. Meaningful improvements in semen analysis results are possible within three to six months when we identify and treat the root causes.

What I recommend depends entirely on what the workup shows. Treatment may include antioxidant support, hormone optimization, thyroid treatment, lifestyle changes, or referral to urology for varicocele repair. For infections or inflammation in the reproductive tract, targeted treatment often improves sperm function significantly.

Lifestyle changes are not a footnote here. Heat avoidance, reducing alcohol, eliminating tobacco and recreational drugs, reducing exposure to plastics and pesticides, and improving sleep all have meaningful evidence behind them and can move the needle considerably.

In My Practice

I ask every person who comes in for a fertility concern to get a sperm evaluation at the same time their partner is being worked up. Not after six months of workups on the other side. At the same time. Fertility is a shared biology, and a thorough sperm workup requires a single blood draw and a semen analysis. There's no reason to wait.

A Note on Testosterone and Fertility

For anyone on testosterone replacement therapy or considering it, fertility is a conversation that needs to happen. Exogenous testosterone suppresses the pituitary signaling that drives sperm production. Most people on standard TRT produce little to no sperm while on treatment.

This doesn't mean testosterone therapy is off the table. There are protocols that support testosterone levels while preserving or restoring fertility. But they require a different approach than standard TRT, and the earlier that conversation happens, the more options are available.

This applies to anyone on testosterone, including transgender men and gender-diverse people on gender-affirming testosterone therapy who may be considering biological parenthood. If this is your situation and it hasn't come up with your provider yet, bring it up. Options exist, and earlier planning gives you more of them.

When to Get Evaluated

If you and your partner have been trying to conceive for 12 months without success, or 6 months if the person carrying is over 35, a sperm workup should happen now. If there's a known history of fertility problems, varicocele, hormonal issues, or significant medication use, earlier evaluation makes sense.

You don't have to wait for a problem. If you're planning to start trying in the next year, a baseline workup gives you time to address anything before it becomes an obstacle. For anyone navigating fertility alongside hormone therapy, a conversation sooner rather than later is always the right move.
