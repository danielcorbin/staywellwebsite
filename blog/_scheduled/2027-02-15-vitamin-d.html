Vitamin D: Why "Normal" Isn't Always Enough

Daniel Corbin, DNP, APRN, FNP-C February 15, 2027 6 min read

Vitamin D is one of the most commonly tested nutrients in primary care. It's also one of the most commonly misread. If your level came back "normal," you may have been told there's nothing to address. But normal on a standard lab range and optimal for your health are not the same thing.

What Vitamin D Actually Does

Calling it a vitamin is a bit of a misnomer. Vitamin D behaves more like a hormone. It binds to receptors found in nearly every tissue in the body, including the brain, immune cells, gut, heart, and muscle. It plays a role in calcium absorption and bone density, yes, but also in immune regulation, mood, insulin sensitivity, and inflammation.

When levels are low, the downstream effects are wide. That's part of why research has linked low Vitamin D to everything from autoimmune conditions and depression to increased infection risk and poor metabolic health. The associations aren't perfect, but the biology is consistent.

The Problem with "Normal"

Standard lab ranges for Vitamin D (25-hydroxyvitamin D) typically flag deficiency below 20 ng/mL and insufficiency below 30 ng/mL. Those cutoffs were designed around bone health, specifically rickets and osteomalacia. They were never intended to represent the level optimal for immune function, mood regulation, or metabolic health.

Most functional medicine practitioners, and a growing body of research, point to 50 to 80 ng/mL as the range associated with broader health benefits. Many patients I see are sitting at 32 or 38 ng/mL, well within "normal" on a standard panel, and feel significantly better once their levels are optimized.

Who's at Risk

Low Vitamin D is remarkably common, particularly in the American South, which surprises people. Sun exposure is the primary way the body produces Vitamin D, but several factors blunt that.

Skin tone matters. Melanin reduces Vitamin D synthesis, meaning people with darker skin produce less from the same sun exposure. Geography and season matter. Even in sunny climates, the angle of sunlight in winter months significantly reduces UVB penetration. Sunscreen use, indoor lifestyles, obesity (Vitamin D is sequestered in fat tissue), and certain medications, including some cholesterol drugs and antiepileptics, can all drive levels down.

Age is also a factor. The skin's ability to synthesize Vitamin D decreases as we get older, and kidney function, which activates the final form of Vitamin D, also declines with age.

Getting Your Levels Up

The most natural way to raise Vitamin D is sunlight. When UVB rays hit your skin, your body synthesizes Vitamin D3 directly. Midday sun (roughly 10am to 2pm) is the most effective window, and you don't need much: 15 to 30 minutes of direct skin exposure on the arms and legs a few times a week can meaningfully move levels for many people. Longer times are needed for people with darker skin tones or those at higher latitudes.

The practical challenge is that most people aren't getting that consistently, especially in fall and winter, with desk jobs, sunscreen use, and time constraints. That's where supplementation fills the gap.

If you do supplement, Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) is the preferred form, more effective than D2 at raising and maintaining serum levels. It's best taken with a fat-containing meal for absorption, and it works in tandem with Vitamin K2, which helps direct calcium into bones rather than soft tissue. If you're taking higher doses of D3, K2 is worth including.

Dosing depends on how low your levels are and how your body responds. I typically recheck levels in 90 days after starting to see how someone is converting. Some people need more than others, and there's no substitute for the follow-up lab to confirm you've hit the target range.

The Bottom Line

Vitamin D is one of the simplest things to test and, when low, one of the simplest things to address. But you have to know where you actually stand, not just whether you're above the floor. If your last panel said "normal" and nobody followed up, it may be worth taking a closer look.
