Food Sensitivities vs. Food Allergies: What's the Difference and Why It Matters

Daniel Corbin, DNP, APRN, FNP-C March 15, 2027 6 min read

Bloating. Fatigue. Brain fog. Joint pain. Skin flares. These are some of the most common complaints I hear in practice, and they're also some of the most frustrating to track down. When standard labs come back normal and symptoms persist, food often comes up as a possible cause. But the conversation about food reactions is muddier than it should be, partly because the terms get used interchangeably when they actually describe very different things.

Here's how to think about them clearly.

What a Food Allergy Is

A true food allergy is an IgE-mediated immune response. That means your immune system has tagged a specific protein as a threat, and when you eat it, the response is immediate and systemic. We're talking hives, throat tightening, difficulty breathing, anaphylaxis. The timing is fast, usually within minutes to two hours, and the connection between cause and effect is obvious. Peanuts, tree nuts, shellfish, fish, milk, eggs, wheat, and soy account for the majority of true food allergies.

These are the reactions that require an EpiPen, an allergist, and strict avoidance. They're serious, but they're also relatively rare and usually well-identified.

What a Food Sensitivity Is

Food sensitivities are a different category entirely. Rather than IgE, they involve IgG antibodies, and the immune response is slower and more diffuse. Symptoms can appear anywhere from two to 72 hours after eating a reactive food, which makes the connection nearly impossible to track without a structured approach.

The symptom picture is also broader. Food sensitivities have been associated with bloating and other digestive symptoms, fatigue and low energy, brain fog and difficulty concentrating, joint pain and stiffness, skin issues including eczema and acne, headaches and migraines, and mood changes and disrupted sleep. Because these symptoms are nonspecific and delayed, most people never connect them to something they ate, especially if the food in question is something they eat regularly.

That's one of the more counterintuitive aspects of food sensitivities: the foods you eat most often are frequently the ones driving symptoms. Regular exposure to a reactive food can create a constant low-grade inflammatory state rather than an obvious acute reaction.

A Note on Food Intolerances

Food intolerances are worth distinguishing from sensitivities because they work through a completely different mechanism. Lactose intolerance, for example, is not an immune reaction at all. It's an enzyme deficiency. People with lactose intolerance lack sufficient lactase to break down the sugar in dairy, and the result is fermentation, gas, and GI discomfort. Similar mechanisms are at play with fructose malabsorption and histamine intolerance.

These are real and worth identifying, but they're digestive issues rather than immune-mediated responses. The treatment approach is different: managing dose and enzyme support rather than immune modulation.

How to Actually Identify a Sensitivity

The gold standard for identifying food sensitivities remains the elimination diet. The standard protocol removes the most commonly reactive foods, including gluten, dairy, eggs, soy, corn, peanuts, alcohol, and added sugar, for four to six weeks. After the elimination phase, foods are reintroduced one at a time, every three to four days, with careful attention to symptoms. When done correctly, this process identifies reactive foods with a level of precision that no lab test currently matches.

IgG food sensitivity panels can serve as a useful starting point. They measure antibody levels to hundreds of foods and can help narrow down what to focus on during an elimination. But IgG antibodies can also reflect recent exposure rather than true reactivity, and results require clinical interpretation. I use them as a tool to guide the process, not as a definitive answer on their own.

What often underlies food sensitivities, particularly when someone is reacting to a wide range of foods, is increased gut permeability, sometimes called leaky gut. When the intestinal lining is compromised, food particles can cross into the bloodstream before they're fully broken down, triggering immune responses that wouldn't happen in a healthy gut. Healing the gut lining is often the root-cause work that makes the most lasting difference. That involves removing inflammatory foods, supporting the microbiome, and addressing the factors, including stress, poor sleep, and certain medications, that degrade the gut barrier over time.

The Bottom Line

Food sensitivities are common, underdiagnosed, and genuinely worth investigating when symptoms are persistent and unexplained. They're not the same as allergies, and testing alone isn't enough to sort them out. The process takes time and structure, but identifying reactive foods and addressing root-cause gut issues can make a meaningful difference in how you feel day to day.
