What Dry January Actually Does to Your Body

January 15, 2027 By Daniel Corbin, DNP, APRN, FNP-C 6 min read

Every January, a lot of people decide to take a month off from alcohol. Some do it as a reset after the holidays. Some are curious what a break might feel like. Some have been thinking about their relationship with drinking for a while and want to test it.

Whatever the reason, the biology is the same. When you stop drinking, your body starts doing things it hasn't been able to do, often for years. And a lot of those things show up faster than people expect.

Your sleep gets better, but not right away.

Alcohol is sedating, which is why a lot of people use it to wind down. But sedation is not the same thing as sleep. Alcohol suppresses REM sleep, the stage where your brain consolidates memory, processes emotion, and does most of its repair work. You may fall asleep faster after a drink, but the sleep you get is shallower and less restorative.

When you stop drinking, the first few nights can actually feel worse. Your nervous system is recalibrating. But by week two, most people are sleeping more deeply and waking up feeling more rested than they have in a long time. That improvement in sleep quality is one of the most consistent things I hear from patients who take a break.

Your liver starts catching up.

Your liver is responsible for metabolizing alcohol, and it does that job at the expense of everything else it needs to do. When alcohol is present, the liver prioritizes breaking it down over regulating blood sugar, processing hormones, filtering toxins, and producing the proteins your body needs.

Even moderate, regular drinking creates a backlog. When you stop, the liver gets to work on all of it. Studies show measurable reductions in liver enzymes within just a few weeks of abstaining. If your ALT or AST has ever come back elevated on labs, a month off alcohol is one of the most effective things you can do to bring those numbers down.

Inflammation drops.

Alcohol is inflammatory. It disrupts the gut lining, alters the microbiome, and triggers an immune response that raises systemic inflammation markers. If you have joint pain, skin issues, brain fog, or just feel generally puffy and off, alcohol is worth looking at as a contributor.

Within a few weeks of stopping, most people see a meaningful reduction in that baseline inflammatory load. Joints feel better. Skin clears up. The mental clarity that comes back surprises a lot of people, because they had stopped noticing how much the alcohol was affecting it.

Your hormones shift.

Alcohol affects testosterone, estrogen, cortisol, and insulin, all at once. In men, regular drinking suppresses testosterone and raises estrogen. In women, it raises estrogen and disrupts the hormonal balance that regulates the menstrual cycle. For anyone managing weight, blood sugar, or mood, alcohol is quietly working against you in the background.

A month off gives your endocrine system a chance to rebalance. It is not a complete fix if something is off, but it removes a significant variable. That is actually one of the reasons I find Dry January clinically useful: it separates what alcohol is doing from what everything else is doing, and sometimes what comes back into balance tells you something important.

You learn something about your habits.

This is the part that isn't strictly biology, but it matters. A month without alcohol shows you where it was sitting in your routine. The glass of wine while cooking dinner. The drink to decompress after a hard day. The social situations where you reached for it without really thinking about it.

None of that is automatically a problem. But knowing it is there gives you a choice. And that is different from just going through the motions without noticing.

What if you're not doing Dry January?

That's fine too. This isn't about abstinence as a goal. It's about understanding what alcohol is actually doing in your body so you can make decisions that align with how you want to feel.
