What I Wish Every Patient Knew at the Start of the Year

January 1, 2027 By Daniel Corbin, DNP, APRN, FNP-C 7 min read

Every January, something shifts. People come in with more intention than usual. They want to feel better, lose weight, get their labs checked, finally deal with the thing they've been putting off. That energy is real, and I love seeing it.

But I also see what happens by February. The motivation fades, the old patterns creep back, and people feel worse about themselves than they did in December because now they've "failed" on top of everything else.

So before you set a single resolution this year, I want to share the things I wish more of my patients understood walking in. Not rules. Not a program. Just the perspective that actually changes things long-term.

Your labs are a conversation, not a verdict.

Getting bloodwork done is one of the best things you can do for your health. But a lot of people avoid it because they're scared of what they might find out. Here's how I think about it: a number on a lab report doesn't create a problem. It just tells you whether one already exists. Finding out early gives you options. Finding out late takes them away.

If your labs have come back "normal" and you still feel off, that's also worth investigating. Normal doesn't always mean optimal, and your symptoms are data too.

Fatigue is not a personality trait.

This is probably the thing I say most often to new patients. Feeling tired all the time is not just who you are. It's not an inevitable consequence of being an adult with responsibilities. Persistent fatigue is a symptom. It has causes. And most of those causes are findable and fixable.

The same goes for brain fog, low mood, poor sleep, and that general feeling of running on empty. These things are so common that people assume they're normal. They're not. Common and normal are very different things.

Your body has been keeping score.

The habits you've built over the past decade, how you sleep, what you eat, how much stress you carry, whether you move your body, all of it is showing up somewhere in your biology right now. Some of it shows up in labs. Some of it shows up in how you feel day to day. Some of it won't show up for another ten years.

I'm not saying this to create anxiety. I'm saying it because the reverse is also true. The good things you do compound just as much as the harmful ones. A year of better sleep, less sugar, more movement, and lower chronic stress makes a measurable difference in your blood work, your hormones, your inflammation levels, and how you feel waking up every morning.

It doesn't have to be dramatic. It just has to be consistent.

Sustainable beats perfect every single time.

The patients I've seen make the most meaningful progress over the years are almost never the ones who went all-in on January 1st. They're the ones who made one or two changes they could actually stick to and built from there.

A salad every day beats a juice cleanse for a week. Eight hours of sleep beats a 5 AM workout you resent and abandon by February. A 20-minute walk three times a week beats a gym membership you stop using by March.

Your body doesn't reward perfection. It rewards consistency.

A good primary care provider should feel like a partner.

One of the biggest things I see holding people back from better health is a bad experience with the medical system. They felt rushed. Their concerns were dismissed. They were handed a prescription and shown the door. And so they stopped going.

What I want you to know is that it doesn't have to work that way. A good provider should listen to you, explain your labs in a way that actually makes sense, and work with you on the whole picture, not just whatever gets your cholesterol in range. If you've never had that experience, it exists. And you deserve it.

This year can actually be different.

Not because of willpower. Not because of the right supplement or the perfect diet. Because you understand a little more about what's driving how you feel, and you have a plan that fits your actual life.
