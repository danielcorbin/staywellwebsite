PCOS Is Not Just a Fertility Problem

Daniel Corbin, DNP, APRN, FNP-C March 1, 2027 7 min read

Most people first hear about PMOS in the context of fertility. And while Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome, formerly known as Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS), is one of the leading causes of ovulatory infertility, that framing misses most of what it actually is. PMOS is a metabolic and hormonal condition that affects nearly every system in the body, and it doesn't stop mattering once someone is done trying to conceive.

What PMOS Actually Is

PMOS is defined by a combination of three features: irregular or absent ovulation, elevated androgens (either measured in blood or visible through symptoms like acne and excess hair growth), and polycystic-appearing ovaries on ultrasound. You don't need all three to be diagnosed. The Rotterdam criteria, the most widely used diagnostic framework, requires only two of the three.

What makes PMOS complicated is that it looks different from person to person. Some people have all three features. Some have irregular cycles but no visible androgen symptoms. Some have elevated androgens with regular cycles. The phenotype variation is wide, which is part of why it's underdiagnosed and why people often spend years without answers.

The Insulin Connection

Here's what most people with PMOS aren't told: insulin resistance is present in roughly 70 to 80 percent of cases, regardless of body weight. This is not a consequence of PMOS. For most people, it's a core driver of it.

Elevated insulin stimulates the ovaries to produce more androgens. Those androgens disrupt follicle development and suppress ovulation. The result is the hormonal cascade that defines the condition. Address the insulin resistance and the androgen excess often improves. That's why some of the most effective interventions for PMOS, including certain medications, dietary patterns, and exercise, work primarily through improving insulin sensitivity rather than directly targeting hormones.

Long-Term Health Risks

Because PMOS involves chronic insulin resistance and androgen excess, it carries real long-term risks that don't get enough attention. People with PMOS have significantly higher rates of type 2 diabetes, often developing it decades earlier than the general population. They have higher rates of metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and endometrial cancer (from chronic anovulation and unopposed estrogen).

They also have higher rates of anxiety, depression, and sleep disorders, which may be related to the hormonal environment, the burden of a chronic condition, or both.

None of this is inevitable. But it requires knowing about it, and it requires a provider who's managing PMOS as the metabolic condition it is rather than just treating individual symptoms.

What Management Actually Looks Like

The standard of care often stops at the birth control pill and metformin. Those can be appropriate tools, but they're not the whole picture and they're not right for everyone.

Effective functional medicine management of PMOS looks at insulin sensitivity through fasting glucose, fasting insulin, and HOMA-IR. It evaluates the full androgen panel, not just total testosterone. It looks at thyroid function, since hypothyroidism mimics and worsens PMOS. It considers inflammatory markers and cortisol, since chronic stress elevates androgens through the adrenal axis.

From there, the interventions are layered. Diet matters: lower glycemic load, adequate protein, and anti-inflammatory foods consistently improve insulin sensitivity and androgen levels. Resistance training is one of the most effective non-pharmacologic tools available. Sleep and stress management are not optional add-ons; they directly affect cortisol and insulin. Targeted supplements, including inositol (particularly the myo and D-chiro combination), berberine, and magnesium, have meaningful evidence behind them.

The Bottom Line

PMOS is a lifelong condition, but it's also one of the most responsive to the right kind of management. The key is treating the whole picture, not just the most visible symptom in front of you at any given visit. Whether you've been diagnosed and want a more complete picture, or you've had symptoms for years without answers, that conversation is worth having.
