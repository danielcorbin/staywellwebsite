Your Blood Sugar Numbers Came Back Normal. Here's Why That Might Not Mean What You Think.

December 15, 2026 By Daniel Corbin, DNP, APRN, FNP-C 8 min read

A patient comes in feeling exhausted, foggy, and dependent on sugar and caffeine to get through the day. They had labs done recently. Everything came back normal. Their provider told them they were fine.

But they're not fine. And often, their blood sugar numbers are part of the story, even if no one flagged them.

Fasting glucose and A1C are two of the most commonly ordered labs, and they're also two of the most misread. The standard reference ranges used in most labs are designed to catch disease, not to identify dysfunction. By the time something gets flagged as abnormal, the problem has usually been building for years.

Here's what these numbers actually mean, and what to look for in your own results.

What Is Fasting Glucose?

Fasting glucose is a snapshot. It measures how much sugar is in your blood after you've gone at least eight hours without eating. When you fast overnight and go in for morning labs, that number is your fasting glucose.

In a healthy body, your pancreas releases insulin after meals to move glucose out of your blood and into your cells, where it gets used for energy. By morning, blood sugar should be low and steady.

When fasting glucose starts creeping up, it usually means one of two things. Either your body isn't producing enough insulin, or your cells have stopped responding to it properly. That second scenario is called insulin resistance, and it's one of the most common and underdiagnosed conditions I see.

Here's what the standard ranges look like versus what I actually aim for in practice:

Under 100 mg/dLTechnically "normal" by standard labs, but anything in the 90s still warrants attention

A fasting glucose of 95 isn't going to get flagged, but it tells me something is happening. "Normal" just means you haven't crossed a threshold. It doesn't mean you're in a good place.

What Is A1C?

If fasting glucose is a snapshot, A1C is more like a three-month average. It measures the percentage of your red blood cells that have glucose stuck to them. Because red blood cells live for about 90 days, A1C gives you a window into how your blood sugar has been running over time, not just on the morning you got your labs drawn.

This makes it a more reliable marker than a single fasting glucose reading. Someone can have a perfectly normal fasting glucose and still have an elevated A1C if their blood sugar is spiking significantly after meals.

A1CStandard Classification
Under 5.7%"Normal" by standard labs
5.7% to 6.4%Prediabetes
6.5% or aboveDiabetes
Under 5.4%Optimal (functional range)

An A1C of 5.6% is technically normal. But in functional medicine, we consider anything above 5.3 to 5.4% a sign that blood sugar regulation deserves attention. That's especially true if it's creeping up year after year.

What Insulin Resistance Actually Feels Like

Here's what surprises most people. Blood sugar dysfunction often doesn't show up on labs until it's been going on for years. The underlying problem, insulin resistance, can be present for a decade before fasting glucose or A1C move into the prediabetic range.

But the body is giving signals the whole time. The symptoms of insulin resistance are things most people chalk up to stress, aging, or just life:

  • Fatigue after meals, especially carb-heavy ones
  • Strong cravings for sugar or starchy foods, particularly in the afternoon
  • Difficulty losing weight, especially around the midsection
  • Energy crashes two to three hours after eating
  • Brain fog that gets worse as the day goes on
  • Trouble staying asleep, particularly in the second half of the night
  • Skin tags or darkening skin in the creases of your neck or armpits

If that list sounds familiar, it doesn't necessarily mean you have a blood sugar problem. But it means it's worth looking more closely at your metabolic health.

What I Look at Beyond the Standard Labs

Fasting glucose and A1C are a starting point, not the whole picture. When I'm evaluating someone's metabolic health, I also look at fasting insulin, which is often the earliest marker of insulin resistance and rarely ordered in standard panels. I look at triglycerides and HDL cholesterol, which tell me a lot about how well the body is handling carbohydrates. And I often look at a two-hour glucose tolerance test if someone has symptoms but normal fasting numbers.

The goal isn't to find disease. The goal is to catch the direction things are heading while there's still plenty of room to turn it around.

What You Can Actually Do

Blood sugar regulation responds well to lifestyle changes, often more quickly than people expect. The biggest levers are what you eat, when you eat, how much you move, and how well you sleep.

Eating protein and fat with carbohydrates slows glucose absorption and reduces post-meal spikes. A short walk after meals, even 10 minutes, significantly improves glucose clearance. Prioritizing sleep matters more than most people realize. And managing chronic stress, which keeps cortisol elevated and blood sugar with it, is often the missing piece.

None of this requires a prescription. But knowing where you actually stand is the first step, which means looking at your numbers with fresh eyes instead of just accepting "normal" as good enough.
