Sleep Apnea Is More Than a Snoring Problem

October 1, 2026 · 10 min read

Most people think of sleep apnea as a snoring issue. Something their partner complains about. An annoyance, not a real medical problem. That's a dangerous misconception. Untreated sleep apnea quietly damages your heart, disrupts your hormones, wrecks your metabolism, and accelerates brain aging. I've seen it drive conditions that patients had been struggling with for years. The good news: it's diagnosable with a simple home test, and treating it changes everything.

What Sleep Apnea Actually Is

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) happens when the muscles in your throat relax too much during sleep. Your airway narrows or closes completely, you stop breathing for 10 to 60 seconds, your brain sounds the alarm, you partially wake up just enough to resume breathing, and the cycle starts again. This can happen 5, 30, even 100 times per hour, all night long.

You're probably not aware of it. Most people with sleep apnea don't recall waking up. They just feel exhausted, foggy, or "off," and they have no idea why.

Common Signs You Might Have Sleep Apnea

  • Loud or irregular snoring (especially with gasping or choking sounds)
  • Waking up with a headache or dry mouth
  • Falling asleep easily during the day, even when you slept a full night
  • Difficulty concentrating or remembering things
  • Mood changes, irritability, or depression
  • Waking frequently to urinate at night (nocturia)
  • High blood pressure that's hard to control
  • Your partner tells you that you stop breathing in your sleep

Sleep apnea is far more common than most people realize. Estimates suggest 1 in 5 adults has at least mild OSA, and the majority are undiagnosed.

What Happens to Your Body When You Stop Breathing All Night

Every time you have an apnea event, your oxygen levels drop. Your body interprets this as an emergency. It floods your system with stress hormones, spikes your blood pressure, and jolts your nervous system out of restorative sleep. Over months and years, this repeated stress takes a serious toll.

Your Heart Takes the Worst of It

Sleep apnea is one of the strongest independent risk factors for cardiovascular disease. The repeated oxygen drops and blood pressure spikes during the night damage blood vessel walls, promote inflammation, and accelerate arterial stiffening. People with untreated sleep apnea have significantly higher rates of hypertension, heart attack, stroke, atrial fibrillation, and sudden cardiac death.

If you have high blood pressure that doesn't respond well to medication, undiagnosed sleep apnea is one of the first things I look for. It's that common a driver.

Your Metabolism Pays a Price Too

Sleep apnea drives insulin resistance. The cortisol spikes from repeated nighttime awakenings increase blood sugar, and poor sleep quality directly impairs how your cells respond to insulin. Over time, this raises your risk of type 2 diabetes and makes it much harder to lose weight, even when your diet and exercise are dialed in.

Here's something worth understanding: insulin resistance and sleep apnea feed each other. Excess weight makes apnea worse, and apnea drives the hormonal dysfunction that makes weight gain easier and weight loss harder. Treating one helps the other.

If you have blood pressure that won't budge, or weight you can't lose despite doing everything right, untreated sleep apnea may be the missing piece.

Your Hormones Get Disrupted

Deep, restorative sleep is when your body produces most of its growth hormone. Growth hormone is critical for muscle repair, fat metabolism, and cellular regeneration. Fragmented sleep from sleep apnea suppresses growth hormone release significantly.

In men, sleep apnea is one of the most underappreciated causes of low testosterone. The testes need good sleep to produce testosterone effectively. Studies consistently show that treating sleep apnea raises testosterone levels, sometimes substantially, without any hormone therapy at all.

For women, the hormone picture is more complex. Sleep apnea worsens during menopause as estrogen declines, and it can worsen hot flashes, mood instability, and fatigue in ways that look like pure hormone deficiency. Treating the apnea often improves these symptoms significantly.

Your Brain and Mood Suffer

The brain clears metabolic waste during deep sleep through a system called the glymphatic system. This is essentially your brain's overnight cleaning cycle. When sleep is fragmented by apnea, this cleaning process is interrupted night after night.

The link between sleep apnea and dementia is becoming clearer in the research. Repeated hypoxia (low oxygen) damages brain tissue, and the glymphatic disruption allows amyloid and tau proteins to accumulate. People with untreated sleep apnea have higher rates of cognitive decline and Alzheimer's disease.

In the shorter term, sleep apnea is strongly linked to depression and anxiety. The chronic fatigue, cortisol excess, and oxygen deprivation all affect mood regulation. Many patients who've been treated for depression for years finally feel better after treating previously undiagnosed sleep apnea.

Your HPA Axis Stays in Overdrive

Each apnea event triggers the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis to release cortisol. Do this hundreds of times a night, every night, and you end up with chronically dysregulated cortisol. This is one of the clearest examples of a lifestyle factor driving what people call "adrenal fatigue." The adrenals aren't failing. They're being overworked by a structural problem that nobody addressed.

Treating sleep apnea is one of the most powerful interventions I know for resetting the HPA axis and improving cortisol patterns.

How I Test for Sleep Apnea

You don't need to spend a night in a lab strapped to machines. I order home sleep studies, which are accurate, comfortable, and easy to do. You take a small device home, wear it to bed, and return it the next day. The data captures your oxygen levels, breathing patterns, heart rate, and the number of apnea and hypopnea events per hour (the AHI, or apnea-hypopnea index).

Home sleep testing is appropriate for most people with suspected obstructive sleep apnea. In-lab polysomnography is reserved for specific cases where OSA is unlikely but another sleep disorder (such as periodic limb movement disorder or narcolepsy) is suspected, or when the home study is inconclusive.

Understanding Your AHI Score

  • AHI < 5: Normal
  • AHI 5 to 15: Mild sleep apnea
  • AHI 15 to 30: Moderate sleep apnea
  • AHI > 30: Severe sleep apnea

Even mild sleep apnea is worth treating when symptoms are present. The AHI is a guide, not the whole picture.

Beyond the sleep study itself, I also look at the broader picture. Fasting insulin and HOMA-IR to assess for insulin resistance. Cortisol patterns. Testosterone and other hormones. Inflammatory markers like hs-CRP. Sleep apnea rarely exists in isolation, and treating it effectively means understanding how it fits into the rest of your physiology.

Treatment: What Actually Works

Treatment for sleep apnea is not one-size-fits-all. I work with each patient based on the severity of their apnea, their anatomy, their preferences, and what other health factors are in play.

Weight Loss: The Most Powerful Long-Term Fix

Fat tissue around the neck and throat is a major contributor to airway obstruction. Even a 10% reduction in body weight can reduce AHI by as much as 25 to 30%. Significant weight loss can resolve sleep apnea entirely in many patients.

This is an area where I can do a lot. I address weight through a functional medicine lens: identifying metabolic drivers like insulin resistance, optimizing hormones, building a sustainable nutrition approach, and using medications like GLP-1 receptor agonists when they're appropriate and beneficial. Weight loss for sleep apnea isn't just about looking different. It can literally allow you to breathe normally again.

CPAP: Still the Gold Standard for Moderate to Severe OSA

CPAP (continuous positive airway pressure) works by delivering a steady stream of pressurized air through a mask while you sleep, keeping the airway open. When tolerated, it's extremely effective. It reduces AHI dramatically, lowers blood pressure, improves daytime functioning, and over time, reduces cardiovascular risk.

The challenge is adherence. Many patients struggle with the mask, the noise, or the feeling of pressure. If you've tried CPAP before and stopped, I want to know why. There are now better masks, quieter machines, auto-adjusting pressure (APAP), and heated humidifiers that make a significant difference in comfort. Often it's worth revisiting.

Oral Appliances: A Real Alternative

For patients with mild to moderate sleep apnea, or those who simply cannot tolerate CPAP, mandibular advancement devices (MADs) are a well-validated alternative. These are custom-fitted mouthpieces, made by a dentist who specializes in sleep medicine, that hold the lower jaw slightly forward to keep the airway open.

Oral appliances are not as universally effective as CPAP for severe apnea, but for the right patient they produce excellent results, and patients are far more likely to actually use them. I refer for oral appliance therapy when it's appropriate and help monitor effectiveness with follow-up home sleep testing.

Positional Therapy

Some people have sleep apnea primarily or exclusively when sleeping on their back (supine position). The airway collapses more easily in that position due to gravity. For these patients, positional therapy, meaning strategies to stay on your side, can meaningfully reduce AHI. This can be as simple as a positioning pillow or a wearable device that gently prompts you to shift when you roll onto your back.

Lifestyle: Reducing What Makes Apnea Worse

Several factors make sleep apnea significantly worse and are worth addressing regardless of what other treatment you're using:

  • Alcohol: Relaxes throat muscles, worsens apnea. Especially problematic within a few hours of bedtime.
  • Sedatives and sleep aids: Many common sleep medications relax airway muscles and worsen OSA. If you're taking these, we need to have that conversation.
  • Smoking: Causes airway inflammation and swelling.
  • Nasal congestion: Chronic nasal obstruction increases the effort to breathe and worsens apnea. Treating allergies, nasal polyps, or a deviated septum can matter.
  • Sleep position: Sleeping on your side is almost always better than your back for OSA.

What Changes When You Treat Sleep Apnea

Patients who successfully treat their sleep apnea often describe it as a before-and-after moment in their health. Here's what I see in practice:

  • Energy levels that actually feel normal for the first time in years
  • Blood pressure that finally responds to treatment
  • Testosterone that comes back up without hormone therapy
  • Insulin resistance that improves, making weight loss more achievable
  • Depression and anxiety that lighten significantly
  • Mental clarity and memory that improve over weeks and months
  • A partner who can actually sleep in the same room

This is why I take sleep apnea seriously even when patients dismiss it. It is not a minor inconvenience. It is a systemic condition that affects nearly every organ and hormone system in your body. Treating it is one of the highest-leverage things you can do for your long-term health.

My Approach in Practice

When I suspect sleep apnea, I order a home sleep study directly. No referrals, no waiting list, no sleeping in a lab. If it's positive, we talk through all treatment options, including what's most likely to work for your specific anatomy and lifestyle. I also look at the downstream effects: your hormones, your metabolic health, your cardiovascular risk. Sleep apnea is rarely just a sleep problem, and treating it well means treating the whole picture.

When to Get Tested

You don't need to be severely overweight or snore loudly to have sleep apnea. Thin people get it. Women get it (and are often under-diagnosed). Athletes get it. If you wake up unrefreshed, struggle with daytime fatigue, have high blood pressure that's hard to control, or your partner notices anything unusual about your breathing at night, that's enough reason to get a home sleep study done.

The test is easy. The information is valuable. And if it's positive, treatment can genuinely transform how you feel.
